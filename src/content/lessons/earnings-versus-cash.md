## What it means
Earnings summarize accounting performance; cash flow concerns money arriving and leaving. Sey uses EBITDA as an initial screen because it looks before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization. That makes it a useful discussion starting point, but not a claim that every pound of EBITDA is available to repay acquisition borrowing or distribute to owners. [E064: original discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1C5gI6LlWhA)

Adjusted EBITDA adds another question: what level of earnings can reasonably continue under the proposed ownership? The adjustment process should make the business more understandable. It should not become a way to increase the headline figure by removing expenses that remain necessary. [E035: original discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vkKw1MeUors)

## Why it matters
An acquisition can be profitable while having little room for cash demands. Principal repayments, seller installments and investment in operations compete for the same available money. The collection's most concrete financing illustration leaves a small remainder after scheduled payments, even though the starting earnings appear substantial. [E216: original discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VmRqlJuqCs8)

Sey also describes different capital-investment needs across steel and HVAC businesses. That comparison is his experience, not a universal sector rule. Its educational value is to ask what the specific company must spend to preserve its earnings. Depreciation being excluded from EBITDA does not answer the replacement-spending question. [E147: original discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SPMaYlRzACI)

## How it works
Begin with the accounts and state the exact earnings measure and period. Then list proposed adjustments with an explanation and evidence. If an owner performs work for which the accounts show little salary expense, investigate the cost of replacing that work. In the Irish target discussion, the proposed earnings normalization includes operator compensation before a valuation multiple is applied. [E175: original discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=akgHprIWcZU)

Next build a separate cash schedule. Show scheduled bank principal, interest and seller payments, and identify taxes, capital expenditure and working-capital movements still missing from a simplified model. This is an editorial extension of the source's debt-service calculation, whose remainder is not a complete measure of distributable cash. [E216: original discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VmRqlJuqCs8)

## Practical interpretation
For every add-back, ask what actually changes after acquisition. Who stops receiving the money? What activity stops? Could revenue suffer? A marketing expense is not automatically unnecessary because a buyer dislikes it. Sey uses an illustration in which removing £0.5m of marketing turns £0.5m of actual earnings into a claimed £1m; his objection is that continued sales have not been demonstrated. [E035: original discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vkKw1MeUors)

Keep a downside case alongside the adjusted figure. The point is not to invent a more impressive spreadsheet, but to see whether small operating disappointments consume the cash left after fixed obligations. This follows directly from the narrow remainder in his repayment example. [E216: original discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VmRqlJuqCs8)

## A worked example
**Source hypothetical, with stated omissions.** Start with £1m of earnings and a £3.5m price. Funding consists of £2.5m bank debt, £0.5m seller financing and £0.5m equity. Five-year straight-line bank principal is £500,000 annually. Using the source's illustrative 10% first-year interest assumption gives £250,000 interest; £100,000 goes to the seller. The remainder is £150,000 before omitted cash adjustments. [E216: original discussion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VmRqlJuqCs8)

That arithmetic explains payment pressure. It does not establish current interest rates or a complete cash-flow forecast. The transcript's explanation of LIBOR is questionable and is not used here as a description of today's lending benchmarks. The rate is retained solely as an explicit calculation assumption.

## Common mistakes
Do not swap pretax profit, EBITDA and cash flow without showing the bridge. Do not treat a preferred add-back as agreed with the seller or lender. And do not label the £150,000 remainder above a dividend budget: the source calculation leaves important operating cash demands unmodeled.
